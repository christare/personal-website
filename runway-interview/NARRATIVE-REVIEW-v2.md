## The actual writing problem

The earlier script often named the mechanism instead of expressing what the speaker needed. “My access won't open it” sounded like a system description. “You came back” explained an action both characters had just experienced. Cutting those lines down further removed the relationship rather than repairing the dialogue.

The revised approach keeps the information but gives the speaker a reason to say it. Alex needs a reply, a door opened, a difficult conversation postponed, or the radio kept on. The player can respond to those needs, rather than just select a location.

| Moment | Revised line | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trapped | “I'm locked in the lab. My keycard keeps flashing red.” | A concrete failed attempt to get out, expressed in ordinary language. |
| Departure | “{name}? Was that you taking off?” | Alex reaches a frightening conclusion and asks the player to contradict it. |
| Abandonment | “Something's moving in here. Please come back. I need you.” | The danger intrudes on a request for help; consistent with the existing moving-chair footage. |
| Brought aboard | “Just get us out of here. I'll explain. I promise.” | Relief remains tangled with avoidance. The air warning interrupts that avoidance. |
| Leaving after the truth | “Okay. Just don't hang up.” | Alex retreats from asking for rescue to asking for company. |
| Rescue | “I'm here.” / “Of course.” | Direct responses remain simple. They do not need decoration. |

## Critical assessment of the current structure

**The sealed capsule is still an unusually clean solution.** Once independent air and a working rail appear, the player has a clearly safer rescue method. Leaving at that point is chiefly a decision about fear, trust or self-preservation, not an evenly balanced ethical dilemma. Stronger dialogue makes that choice emotionally legible; it cannot create a cost that the plot does not contain. I have not invented a cost or a failure chance.

**The admission does not receive a direct player response.** Alex admits hiding the result, and the story moves to transfer logistics. The later choice carries the response indirectly. A future pass could add one short player acknowledgment before the capsule question, but that would be a new beat and is not part of this pass.

**Some narration is necessary under the no-people rule.** Opening the door and getting Alex aboard happen off screen. A short bridge establishes that two people reached the shuttle. Removing it entirely would make the exposure warning feel disconnected from the player's action. The final captions can be more specific because the geography is already clear.

**The contamination ending establishes exposure and exclusion, not confirmed death.** “You hold your breath. It doesn't help.” expresses an involuntary, futile response without inventing a fatal diagnosis. A reviewer should not casually change this to “you both die” unless the plot is deliberately revised.

**The environmental horror remains ambiguous.** The chair and capsule movement make the threat tangible while keeping it unseen. The story does not explain a telekinetic organism, possession, or what is inside Alex. Adding a biological explanation to justify every image would likely cost more than it adds in this short format.

## Highest-value changes to consider next — not implemented

1. **Let the ending land before grading it.** The ending name currently appears on entry, before the last line. Delaying that label and credits until after the emotional beat would reduce the feeling of receiving a game result while the scene is still playing.
2. **Give the admission one response.** A brief, restrained acknowledgment from the player could make choosing to help after the lie feel earned. It should express hurt or a decision, not repeat that Alex is infected. Add it only if the read-through exposes a real gap.
3. **If a harder moral dilemma is wanted later, establish one visible cost for the capsule.** For example, a transfer that takes time while the station visibly deteriorates. The cost must be shown before the choice and supported by the footage. Do not add an unexplained failure percentage or fake timer.

I would not add a loop for this demo. Irreversible departure supports the emotional premise. Replay already permits exploration. Backtracking makes sense only before a real commitment, and would need to avoid letting the player learn future consequences and then erase the decision inside the same story.

## Review method and craft sources

The line pass tests whether a character would say a line to this listener in this moment, whether the information can be carried by the image or system text, and whether each exchange changes pressure or the relationship. It also checks natural rhythm and whether a character responds to the preceding line rather than merely taking a turn. These are working criteria, not a claim that every short line must be memorable.

- John August, [Dialogue versus exposition](https://johnaugust.com/2004/dialogue-versus-exposition): distinguish the character's reason to speak from the writer's need to deliver information.
- John August, [How to write dialogue](https://johnaugust.com/2007/how-to-write-dialogue): listen for actual speech rhythm, account for the listener, and test whether characters respond to one another.
- John August, [How to write a scene](https://johnaugust.com/2007/write-scene): identify what a scene must accomplish and what would be lost if it were omitted.
